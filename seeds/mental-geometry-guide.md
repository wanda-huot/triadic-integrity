## The Core Provocation

Most conceptual frameworks come with a shape. But people rarely choose the shape deliberately. They reach for whatever form feels familiar — usually a triangle, because triangles feel elegant, or a 2x2, because consultants like boxes.

The result: wrong shapes applied to real problems. A spectrum forced onto a multidimensional situation. A triangle used when there is no genuine tradeoff. A 2x2 built to look sophisticated rather than to clarify.

The corrective is simple to state and harder to practice:

> **Choose the shape that matches the pressure.**

Don't use a triangle because three feels elegant. Don't use a 2x2 because consultants like boxes. Ask what kind of problem-shape you are actually facing.

## The Core Shapes

### Spectrum
**Use when:** one variable dominates, and the question is *more or less*.

A spectrum is for degree, not kind. Hot/cold. Tight/loose. Simple/complex. Fast/slow.

The spectrum is the most common shape and the most overused. Whenever you find yourself disagreeing about whether something is "high" or "low," check whether you are actually arguing about the same variable, or whether you have confused two separate dimensions.

*When the spectrum is wrong:* when you need two axes to explain why people who "agree" keep talking past each other.

---

### 2×2 Matrix
**Use when:** there are two genuinely independent dimensions, and confusing them is the source of the problem.

A 2x2 is not for making something look sophisticated. It is for situations where a debate is stuck because people are treating a two-dimensional space as though it were one.

*Classic diagnostic signal:* two people arguing past each other about whether something is "good" or "bad," when in fact one is talking about quality and the other is talking about cost, or one is talking about short-term and the other about long-term.

*When the 2x2 is wrong:* when one dimension is not genuinely independent of the other, or when you're forcing a continuous landscape into four boxes that hide more than they reveal.

---

### Constraint Triangle (Tradeoff)
**Use when:** scarcity or tradeoff is real — you cannot maximize all three at once.

Speed / quality / cost. Cheap / fast / good. Scope / time / resources.

The constraint triangle names a genuine structural tension. Moving toward one vertex pulls you away from the others. The form is honest about limits.

*When the constraint triangle is wrong:* when you are dealing with elements that co-produce a whole rather than trade off against each other. Applying a tradeoff frame to a generative system produces false choices — "should we prioritize quality or continuity?" is the wrong question inside TIF.

---

### Process Loop (Cycle)
**Use when:** feedback matters more than static classification, or when the same sequence repeats over time.

A loop is for systems that feed back into themselves. Plan / do / review. Diverge / converge / decide. Stimulus / response / learning.

The loop foregrounds time and recursion. It asks: what sustains the cycle, what breaks it, and what accumulates across iterations?

*When the loop is wrong:* when the sequence is genuinely linear and not recursive — when the third stage does not actually feed back into the first.

---

### Stack (Levels)
**Use when:** levels interact and the confusion is between what belongs at which level.

A stack is for situations where micro / meso / macro, or principle / mechanism / application, or structure / culture / behavior are all in play but are being conflated.

*Classic diagnostic signal:* an argument that keeps jumping between levels without noticing. "The problem is the incentive structure" and "the problem is individual choices" are often both true and at different stack levels.

*When the stack is wrong:* when the levels do not interact cleanly, or when what looks like a hierarchy is actually a web.

---

### Triadic Integrity Triangle (Ecological)
**Use when:** three different necessities must cohere — and the question is not "which do we sacrifice?" but "what has gone slack, tight, or misdirected?"

This shape is for situations where all three elements are required, none can be traded off against the others, and health is coherence rather than balance.

*Diagnostic signal:* something feels wrong but no single element is the obvious culprit. Quality is fine; contact is fine; but something in the whole isn't working. The ecological form prompts you to look at the relationships, not just the parts.

*When the ecological triangle is wrong:* when there is a real tradeoff — when you genuinely cannot have all three. In that case, use the constraint triangle.

## Choosing a Shape: Diagnostic Questions

| If the question is... | Consider... |
|---|---|
| How much? More or less? | Spectrum |
| Why are people talking past each other? | 2×2 |
| What can't we have all at once? | Constraint triangle |
| What keeps repeating? What breaks the cycle? | Process loop |
| Which level are we actually talking about? | Stack |
| What has gone out of coherence? | Ecological triangle |

---

## What This Guide Is Not

This is not a complete taxonomy of conceptual shapes. Venn diagrams, flow charts, force-field analyses, polarity maps, and many others have not been included. The shapes here are chosen because they recur most often in Framecraft-adjacent thinking and because the distinctions between them are practically useful.

The guide may expand. For now, six shapes and a diagnostic question is enough.
